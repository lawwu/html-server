In a room of 23, a shared birthday is more likely than not

It feels wrong because we picture our own birthday being matched. But the question is whether any two people match — and the number of pairs grows much faster than the number of people.

Each new person must avoid everyone already in the room. The second person dodges one birthday, the third dodges two, the twenty-third dodges twenty-two. Multiply all those small survivals together and the chance that nobody matches decays quickly:

P(no match among n) = 365/365 × 364/365 × … × (365 − n + 1)/365 P(match) = 1 − P(no match)

Twenty-three people make 253 pairs. Each pair matches with probability 1/365. That is roughly what the dashed line counts.

The approximation treats the 253 pairs as independent, which they are not quite, but it is close: 1 − e−253/365 ≈ 50.0% against the exact 50.7%. It also explains the shape. Pairs grow like n²/2, so doubling the room roughly quadruples the chances to collide; by 57 people a match is 99% certain, and by 70 the chance of no match is under 1 in 1,000.

The same arithmetic governs hash collisions and duplicate random IDs: with N possible values, expect the first collision after roughly √N draws, not N.

Assumes 365 equally likely birthdays and ignores leap days; real birthdays cluster slightly by season, which makes matches a little more likely. Simulations use the browser's random number generator.
