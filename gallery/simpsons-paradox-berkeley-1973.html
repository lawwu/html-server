Data science · exploratory analysis

Men were admitted far more often. In most departments, women were.

The 1973 graduate admissions numbers from UC Berkeley's six largest departments are the textbook case of Simpson's paradox: a gap in the aggregate that shrinks — and in four of six departments reverses — once you split by the variable that drives both who applies and who gets in.

1 · The data

4,526 applications to six departments (anonymized A–F in the original study). Counts are applicants and admits; the rate column is admits ÷ applicants.

2 · Department by department

MenWomencircle area ∝ number of applicants

Read the chart top to bottom and two patterns appear. Within a department the two circles sit close together, and the orange one is to the right in A, B, D and F. But the big blue circles cluster at the top, in A and B, where most applicants are admitted, while the big orange circles sit lower, in C and E, where most are turned away.

The mechanism. Department is a confounder: it affects both the outcome (admission rates range from 6% to 64%) and the grouping (women applied disproportionately to the most selective departments). The aggregate rate is a weighted average of department rates, and the two groups use very different weights.

3 · Reweight it yourself

Hold each department's admission rates for women fixed and change only where women apply. At 0% they apply in their actual 1973 mix; at 100% they apply in exactly the men's mix.

4 · Takeaway

  1. An aggregate comparison is only as fair as its weights. Before reading a gap as an effect, ask what else differs between the groups being compared.
  2. Here, standardizing to a common application mix turns a 14-point gap favoring men into a gap of about 7 points favoring women.
  3. Splitting is not automatically "more correct" either: whether you condition on a variable depends on the causal question. The 1975 study asked about the admissions process, so department-level comparison was the right lens; why women applied where they did is a separate question it could not answer.
Data: six largest departments, UC Berkeley graduate admissions, fall 1973 — Bickel, Hammel & O'Connell, "Sex Bias in Graduate Admissions: Data from Berkeley," Science 187 (1975); the same counts ship with R as datasets::UCBAdmissions. All figures computed in-page from those counts.
