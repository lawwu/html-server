Songbird call classifier

5-second windows32 kHz mono12 species4.0 M parametersMacro F1 0.87

Songbird listens to five-second slices of a field recording and says which of twelve common songbirds of eastern and central North America are singing or calling in each. It exists to get people through hours of dawn-chorus audio quickly: a first pass that someone checks, not a record that stands on its own.

Model details

WindowOne five-second slice of a recording. Every count on this card is a count of windows, not of birds.
Input
Mono audio resampled to 32 kHz and cut into 5.0-second windows. Long recordings are scanned in 2.5-second steps, so every moment is heard twice.
Features
Log-mel spectrogram: 128 bands from 150 Hz to 15 kHz, 32 ms frames every 10 ms, so each window becomes a 500 × 128 image.
Network
EfficientNet-B0 convolutional network, 4.0 M parameters. The backbone was pretrained on general environmental sound, then fine-tuned for 40 epochs.
Outputs
Thirteen independent scores from 0 to 1: one for each species in Plate 2 and one for “none of these”. Several species can pass the threshold in the same window.
Threshold
0.50 for every species by default. Scores are calibrated on the validation sites: windows scored about 0.8 held the species about 80% of the time (expected calibration error 0.03).
Augmentation
Background noise mixed in at 0–20 dB SNR, random time shifts, and time and frequency masking.
Size, speed
16 MB at float32, 4.2 MB at int8. About 30 ms per window on a laptop CPU, so an hour of audio takes roughly 45 seconds.
Banding codesThe four-letter abbreviations bird banders and surveyors use, built from the common name: NOCA is NOrthern CArdinal.

Intended use

Songbird is for people who collect more audio than they can listen to: monitoring coordinators, field ecologists, educators and birders. Its output is a list of candidate detections for a person to confirm.

Use it for

  • Sorting long recordings. First-pass labels for audio from autonomous recorders and phones, so listening time goes to the windows that matter.
  • Tracking common species at known sites from season to season, with a person confirming a sample of detections.
  • Learning by ear. Helping new birders check what they heard in their own recordings.

Do not use it for

  • Species outside the twelve, or documenting a rare bird for a records committee.
  • Counting birds or declaring one absent. It reports presence per window: ten windows may be one bird, and silence is not absence.
  • Regulatory or legal decisions such as environmental review, permits or enforcement.
  • Western North America without first checking it against local recordings.
  • Triggering playback or lures. Playing songs at birds can disrupt breeding.
  • Monitoring people. Recorders pick up voices; see the ethical considerations below.

Validated forEastern and central United States and southern Canada · April to July · SNR of 10 dB or better · up to three of the twelve species in a window.

Training data

1,312 hours of labelled audio, or 944,640 windows, from two sources: 21,300 recordings that 1,140 volunteer recordists contributed to a field-recording archive between 2014 and 2025, each having opted in to machine-learning use; and autonomous recorders at 61 monitoring sites, 2019–2025. 902 of those hours contain at least one of the twelve species. The other 410 hours teach “none of these”: wind, rain, streams, roads, insects, frogs, and 40 other bird species, so the class means none of the twelve, not no birds.

Most species were recorded when they sing hardest, in spring and early summer. Carolina Wren, which sings all year, is the only one recorded evenly through the seasons; Dark-eyed Junco, a winter visitor across much of the East, is weighted to the cold months instead.

How the labels were made

  1. Each recordist listed the species they heard in each file.
  2. Files were cut into five-second windows. Two trained annotators independently marked every species they could hear in each window, listening while watching the sonogram (agreement before review: Cohen’s κ 0.86).
  3. A third annotator settled the 4.6% of windows where the two disagreed; 1.2% stayed ambiguous and were dropped.
  4. Audit: 2,000 random windows were labelled again six months later and 97.1% matched. Most mismatches were a faint, distant second species that one pass missed.

Windows with human speech were removed (found automatically, then checked by hand: 0.8% of the audio), as were recordings the archive flags as sensitive species.

How it was split

By place and by person, never by clip. Each monitoring site went wholly to training (41 sites), validation (6) or test (14), and volunteer recordings were split by recordist, so no site or recordist in the test set was heard in training.

Evaluation

Precision and recallPrecision: of the windows the model called NOCA, the share that held one. Recall: of the windows that held a NOCA, the share it found. F1 balances the two.

Hours and windows count a window once for each species in it. A dash means no single confusion above 2% of that species’ windows.

The three weakest species, RBGR, DEJU and CHSP, have the least training audio, and each has a look-alike among the twelve. Little data alone is not fatal: WTSP has less audio than GRCA yet scores 0.91, because its long, pure whistles followed by triplets are unlike anything else in the set.

Similar species: the pairs it confuses

Base rates decide who pays for a confusion. Only 3.6% of robin windows are called grosbeak, but robins outnumber grosbeaks 4.6 to 1 in the test set, so those 101 windows are 74% of the grosbeak’s false alarms. Without them its precision would be 0.92 rather than 0.76.

Songbird also has no way to say “a bird I don’t know”. In 300 windows of Northern Mockingbird, which imitates other species, 64% were given one of the twelve names, most often CARW (23%), NOCA (14%) or GRCA (11%). In 240 windows of Carolina Chickadee recorded where its range meets the Black-capped Chickadee’s, 58% were called BCCH.

Noise

SNRSignal-to-noise ratio: how far the loudest labelled song stands above the background, in decibels. At 0 dB, song and noise are equally loud.

To isolate noise, 2,400 test windows recorded in quiet conditions (SNR 20 dB or better, macro recall 0.93 as recorded) were mixed with field recordings of five kinds of noise at three levels. Low rumble does less harm than its loudness suggests, because wind and traffic sit below most songs. The insect chorus, with most of its energy between 4.5 and 6.5 kHz, does the most, because it lands on the trills: at 5 dB it cuts CHSP’s recall to 0.34. Plate 1 shows one window under each.

Region was checked separately, outside the test set. On 1,900 windows from six Pacific Northwest sites, the five species that also live there (AMRO, SOSP, RWBL, DEJU, CHSP) reached a macro recall of 0.72, against 0.84 for the same five in the East. Song Sparrow fell furthest, from 0.88 to 0.61; its songs vary strongly from region to region.

Limitations and recommendations

  • A closed world of twelve. Every sound is pushed toward one of the twelve names or “none of these”; a look-alike from outside the set usually gets a wrong name rather than a flag.Where Carolina Chickadees, mockingbirds or thrashers are common, confirm BCCH, CARW, NOCA and GRCA detections by ear.
  • Mostly eastern, mostly near people. 93% of the audio comes from the Northeast, the Great Lakes, the Southeast and southern Canada, much of it from parks and preserves near towns where volunteers live. Farmland, large forests and the West are thin, and errors will concentrate there.Before relying on it somewhere new, label about 200 local windows and compare.
  • Songs more than calls. 61% of species-labelled audio is from April to July; outside those months, when most species call rather than sing, macro recall falls from 0.88 to 0.76.Treat autumn and winter results as indicative only.
  • Songs change. Over the past two decades White-throated Sparrows have been adopting a new song ending that spread east across Canada, and training data that ends in 2025 will fall behind changes like it.Re-check recall every year against fresh labelled windows.
  • Faint birds are under-labelled. Annotators sometimes miss a faint second species, the audit’s most common error. Recall here is measured against what annotators heard, so it overstates how many of all birds present the model finds, and some “false alarms” are probably real.For occupancy studies, estimate detection probability directly rather than borrowing these figures.
  • Dense choruses. With four or more of the twelve in one window, the peak minutes around sunrise, macro recall drops to 0.69.Expect missed species at the height of the chorus; later windows from the same morning help.

Ethical considerations

  • Where sensitive species live. A detection log pairs a species, a time and a recorder’s location, and the audio behind it can hold species the model cannot name, including some whose nest sites are kept private to protect them. The released training metadata rounds locations to 0.1° and leaves out recordings the archive flags as sensitive.Generalize coordinates to roughly 10 km before sharing detections or audio, and follow regional rules for sensitive species.
  • People’s voices. Recorders in parks and yards pick up conversations. Speech was removed from the training data, but a deployment will record it again.Run the model on the device or delete raw audio after processing, keep recorders away from places where people expect privacy, and post notice where the law requires it.
  • Disturbing birds. Testing the model by playing recordings at birds can disrupt territorial and nesting birds.Test on recordings, never on live birds.
  • Consent and credit. Volunteer recordings were used only where the recordist opted in to machine-learning use. Recordists are credited by the name they chose in the dataset release and can withdraw recordings from the next version.
  • Quiet over-reliance. A fast, mostly-right model can gradually replace expert listening without anyone deciding it should.Keep a person confirming a random 5% of detections and every detection of RBGR, DEJU and CHSP; conservation decisions should never rest on Songbird’s output alone.

Songbird, its data and every number on this card are invented for a design example; the birds and their songs are real.

Design notes
  • Sonograph paper
  • Plate stock
  • Graphite
  • Pencil
  • Robin’s egg: only what the model says
  • Cardinal: only misses and confusions

Dark mode is “pre-dawn”, when the chorus starts: the same six roles on a blue-black ground, traces glowing pale. Type pairs a condensed grotesque, in capitals for names, codes and numbers (the instrument’s voice), with an old-style book serif for prose and Latin names (the naturalist’s voice). Both are system faces.

Layout: a field-guide species account for a model, with a name and measurements line, prose in a narrow column, plates breaking wider and definitions in the margin.

What the audit moved: cream paper and forest green became cool sonograph grey, with the accent reserved for model output; a bird illustration as the hero became the sonogram the model actually hears; rounded metric cards became a datasheet keyed by banding codes; a plain bar chart of hours became a checklist bar graph by month.
